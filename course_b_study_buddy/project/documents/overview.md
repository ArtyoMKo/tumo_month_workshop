# The Kestrel Project — Overview

The Kestrel Project is a student-run high-altitude balloon programme based at the Vardavar
Science Centre. It was founded in March 2019 by three students who wanted to photograph the
curvature of the Earth without access to a rocket.

## Purpose

Kestrel exists to give students hands-on experience of a complete engineering cycle: design,
build, launch, recovery and analysis, all within a single academic year. Every flight must
carry at least one experiment proposed by a student in their first year on the programme.

## How a flight works

A Kestrel flight has five stages:

1. **Fill** — a latex balloon is filled with helium until it produces 2.4 kg of lift.
2. **Ascent** — the balloon rises at roughly 5 metres per second for about 100 minutes.
3. **Burst** — at around 32,000 metres the balloon expands beyond its limit and bursts.
4. **Descent** — a parachute slows the payload to about 6 metres per second.
5. **Recovery** — the team follows the GPS tracker and retrieves the payload by car and on foot.

A typical flight lasts 2 hours 40 minutes from release to landing, and the payload usually
comes down between 40 and 90 kilometres downwind of the launch site.

## Rules

Kestrel operates under three rules that have never been broken:

- No flight launches without a valid aviation clearance for that day and that site.
- No payload exceeds 2.8 kg in total mass.
- Every flight carries two independent trackers from different manufacturers.

The two-tracker rule was added after Flight 4, and is the reason no payload has been lost
since.
